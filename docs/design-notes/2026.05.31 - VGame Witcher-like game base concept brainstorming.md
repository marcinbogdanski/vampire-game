## 1. Claude

These all work, but they pull your tone in different directions. Quick read on each, plus some you haven't named.

Abandoned castle — strong but generic; it's the default "evil lair" and slightly contradicts your loner. Castles imply a former population, which actually helps you: who lived here, why did they leave, what did your character displace? If the castle has history tied to monsters or the hunts, it earns its place.

Wizard's tower — fits the "studying captured creatures" mechanic best. Vertical space is great for base-building (each floor = a system: holding cells below, study above, living quarters, signal beacon at top). But it reads more scholar than hunter, so lean into the creature your protagonist is rather than the hunting.

Witch's cottage out of town — best for the "removing bad charm" / conversion angle, since that's folk-magic, ritual, herbalism territory. Downside: hard to scale into a big base-building system. Cottages are intimate, not sprawling.

Some others worth considering, given your specific mechanics (containment + study + a creature protagonist who is himself a kind of monster):

- Decommissioned monastery / abbey — remote by design, has cells (literally, for monks → reusable as containment), is near a town it once served, and carries the "sanctioned by humans but no longer trusted" tension that fits a witcher-like outcast perfectly.
- Old border fort / watchtower — built to keep something out. Now your character keeps things in. Strong thematic mirror, plausibly near a trade road/town.
- A repurposed menagerie or old hunting lodge of a dead noble — already had cages and trophy halls; you inherit infrastructure for capture-and-study, which justifies the mechanic in the fiction.

The containment-and-study mechanic is your most distinctive feature, so I'd weight the base toward whatever makes holding living monsters feel native rather than bolted on. That points at the monastery or the menagerie/lodge over the raw castle.

Want me to sketch how base-building floors/wings could map onto one of these, or think through the worldbuilding tension (why does a town tolerate a monster-hunting monster nearby)?

## 6. Claude

Looking at your sketch: the structure reads clearly — settlement as a graph of screens (R as the hub, V/G/F branching off, C as the higher-traffic junction connecting out to forest/castle). This is a good model. Let me push on the part that's strong and flag the part that's underspecified.

What's working: heat is local geography, not a global number. The best thing in your design is implicit and you should make it explicit — different locations react to heat differently and on different curves. The graveyard goes empty (then ghouls) while the square goes hostile. That means heat isn't one slider, it's a wave moving through the settlement, and each node has its own response. That's genuinely good design: it makes the place feel like it's reacting organically, and it gives the player readable spatial information ("the fields emptied out, the edges are getting dangerous") instead of a number. Lean into this. The settlement should feel like it contracts under heat — life pulls inward toward the guarded center, the edges (graveyard, fields, outer road) hollow out first, and the square is the last thing to turn but the most dangerous when it does.

That contraction is also your difficulty curve expressed as geography. Low heat: the whole graph is open, prey is scattered and soft, you can pick edges off quietly. High heat: the soft prey is gone, what remains is either dangerous (guards, groups) or better (a ghoul in the graveyard is a real capture target, not a drunk). So heat doesn't just raise danger — it changes what's worth hunting, pushing you from easy feeds toward the higher-tier captures you actually want. The system makes you provoke the village to get the good prey. That's the apex-predator power loop again, drawn on a map.

The gap: what's the player's actual verb-loop inside a settlement? Your description is mostly about what each node contains, but the interesting question is how the player moves and chooses. Right now it reads as "enter at R, walk to a node, attack or don't." A few things to nail down, because they determine whether settlements are a system or just a set of rooms:

Does moving between nodes cost something (a turn, a night, a hunger tick)? If movement is free, the player just checks every node and picks the best target — no decision. If movement costs a night and prey shifts between nights, then "which node, in what order, before heat rises" becomes a real hunt with planning. I'd make a settlement visit a night, and have each kill/feed raise local heat for the next night, so you're managing a deteriorating situation across multiple visits rather than clearing a static board.

Is heat per-node, per-settlement, or both? Your examples imply settlement-wide (the whole village tenses), but the graveyard-goes-empty-first behavior implies a per-node component too. The clean version: a settlement-level heat that rises with every incident, and each node has a threshold on that meter where its state flips (graveyard flips early/low, square flips late/high). One meter, many thresholds. That gives you the contracting-inward feel without tracking six separate numbers.

On heat decaying over time: fine for v1, but notice it makes settlements infinitely farmable — wait it out, return, repeat. That's acceptable early, but the structure that makes it interesting later is a small permanent residue: heat decays, but each settlement remembers a floor that creeps up over many visits (they've started warding, they hired a hunter who now lives here). So a settlement you've hunted hard becomes permanently harder — eventually a "death wish" town — which is exactly your power-gradient ceiling emerging from play rather than being hand-placed. You don't need this in v1, but design the heat variable so a permanent floor can be added without rework.

The dryad parallel — yes, and it's more than a reskin if you change the topology. A human village contracts toward a guarded square. A dryad grove should react differently in a way that teaches the player something. Idea: the grove has no "guard" node — instead, under heat the spirits/herb-fields themselves change (the magical spring goes dormant, the dryads withdraw into the central tree and the grove's paths literally rearrange or close). Human heat = social/military response (guards, groups, hunters). Fae heat = the place itself turning against you (paths loop, the spring stops giving, the grove gets harder to navigate). Same heat-contracts-the-map principle, expressed through completely different fiction. That contrast is what makes settlement-types feel distinct rather than recolored.

One structural suggestion on the graph itself: your sketch has C (crossroad) as the connector between the settlement and the outside (forest/castle), with R as the internal hub. That's a strong two-tier shape — C is the threshold (high traffic, well-guarded, the dangerous front door) and R is the interior spine. Consider making that explicit as a rule: you can only enter/leave through the threshold node, and the threshold is where heat hits hardest (it's the guarded approach). That means escaping a hot settlement requires passing back through the most dangerous node — a real risk-management beat at the end of a hunt, rather than just blinking back to the castle. It turns "I got greedy, now I have to get out" into actual gameplay.

So, the questions that'll firm this up:

Is a settlement visit a single continuous session, or a night you leave and return to — and does heat rise within a visit (each kill makes the rest of tonight worse) or only between visits? This is the single biggest decision for how settlements play, because it determines whether the tension is "press your luck right now" or "manage a place over time." Which feels closer to what you want?

## 7. Claude

Your action-economy model is good and it answers the earlier question cleanly: a settlement visit is a day, the day is a budget of 5–10 actions, and travel/explore/wait all spend from it. That's the right granularity. A few things fall out of it that are worth making explicit.

Shared heat is the right call — but make the spread directional, not instant. "Someone gets attacked, the whole village goes high heat" is correct in spirit but if it's instant and total you lose the best texture. The more interesting version: an incident sets a village-wide heat level, but each node reacts on a delay based on distance from the incident and from the center. You attack someone at the graveyard (an edge node) — this turn, the graveyard is hot, the adjacent road tenses, but the square across the village hasn't heard yet. Next action/turn, the alarm propagates inward. So heat is shared (one rising meter) but its visible effects ripple outward from where you struck and inward toward the guarded center, over a few actions.

Why this matters mechanically: it makes where you strike first a real decision. Hit the edge and you get a window — a few actions to grab a second target or get out before the village fully tenses. Hit the square (the center) and the whole place flips almost immediately because you did it in front of everyone. That's the press-your-luck tension you want, and it emerges naturally from "shared meter + propagation delay" without you tracking six independent numbers. One meter, but its consequences arrive node-by-node.

This also resolves your independent-vs-shared instinct: you were right that shared makes more sense (a village is a community, word travels), but your pull toward independent was sensing that nodes should feel different — and they do, just through propagation timing and per-node thresholds rather than separate meters. Shared meter, staggered effects. Best of both.

The action economy creates your real tension: the day as a resource you can't refill. With 5–10 actions and travel costing 1, every action spent traveling or waiting is an action not spent hunting, and every hunt raises heat that degrades the remaining actions of the day. That's a tight little pressure cooker: early actions are cheap and safe (cold village, soft prey), late actions are expensive and dangerous (hot village, but you've used your budget and still need to spend actions escaping). The day has a natural arc — arrive cold, hunt, heat rises, extract before you run out of actions or the square turns on you. You don't need to design that arc; it falls out of the numbers. Just make sure leaving costs actions too (travel back through the threshold node), so a greedy player can genuinely get caught with a hot village and not enough actions to get out clean. That's the moment the system is built to create.

The free-encounter-on-entry + explore/wait-to-roll model is solid, with one tuning note. "Explore or wait rolls a potential encounter or gathers resources" is a clean engine. The thing to watch: if waiting reliably produces encounters, the optimal play is to sit in the richest node and spam wait, which collapses your lovely graph into one screen. The fix is to make the node's encounter table deplete or worsen as you work it — the first explore at the fields finds a lone peasant, the second finds nothing or raises heat (you've been lurking too long, someone noticed). So each node gives you a good action or two then pushes you to move on. That keeps the player traversing the graph (spending travel actions, managing the day) instead of camping. Depletion-per-node is what makes the map matter.

So the model, assembled:

A village visit is one day = 5–10 actions. Travel between nodes costs 1, leaving the village costs travel actions through the threshold. Free encounter on entry to a node; explore/wait rolls further encounters but the node depletes (fewer/worse rolls, rising local attention) the longer you work it. Any attack raises a shared village heat meter, but the effects propagate from the strike point outward and toward the guarded center over subsequent actions, so striking the edge buys a window and striking the center flips the place fast. The day ends with an extraction problem: get back through the threshold before heat or your action budget runs out. Heat decays between days (v1), with the hooks in place to add a creeping permanent floor later.

That's a complete, coherent settlement loop, and it's your loop — it expresses the apex-predator-managing-a-deteriorating-hunt fantasy on a small, cheap-to-build graph of single screens.

Two questions to push it one more step:

When heat is high and you choose to leave a node "without revealing yourself" (your earlier idea — decline the encounter), does declining cost an action and/or still nudge heat? Whether not attacking is free or costed determines if stealth is a real resource-management choice or just a safe default. I'd lean toward declining being cheap but not free (you're loitering, lurking — it still draws faint attention), so that even a careful no-kill scouting day has a cost.

And: across days, does the prey you didn't take persist or reshuffle? If you scout a village and spot a fat capture target (a hedge-witch at the graveyard shrine) but run out of actions, is she there tomorrow, or does the world reroll? Persistent named targets turn settlements into planned heists ("I know she's there, I'll come back ready"); a full reshuffle keeps them as repeatable hunting grounds. Which fits the game you want — hunting grounds, or heists?
